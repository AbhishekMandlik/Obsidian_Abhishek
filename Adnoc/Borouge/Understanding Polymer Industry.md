
### Fixed, lumpy supply meets flexible-but inertial demand.
Shutting down and restarting is expensive, a new cracker adds thousands of tons of capacity overnight, we cant add little more supply so industry lurches between shortage and glut.

### Brutally cyclical, cycle is capacity driven, not only demand driven
Demand planners will also have to forecast not just  "How much will customers buy but also that how much capacity will the competitors bring online". Second moves prices more than the first one.

### Derived is a chain of derived demand, several links removed from the end consumer
Ethylene->Polyethylene->Resin sold to a converter-> Film/bottle/pipe->Retailer
While forecasting we miss these mid links. Forecasting error compounds at every link. (Classic Bullwhip effect).

### What actually drives polymer/petrochemical demands?
1. Depends on macro-economical indicators... largely unforecastable.
2. End-use sector mix.
3. Regional shifts.
4. Feedstock economics: naphtha-based cracker in Asia/Europe vs ethane-based crackers in the US.
5. Regulatory/ sustainability shocks: Single use plastic bans, EPR rules, recycled-content mandates -- These show up as a step function demand destruction in specific applications.


## Forecasting/ planning stack in practice...
1. Statistical baseline forecasting -- Time series methods (Moving averages, exponential smoothing, ARIMA) trained on historical shipment data, adjusted for seasonality.
2. Demand Sensing:  Layering a near real time signal (Like order books, customer inventory positions, POS - adjacent data from downstream converters) to correct statistical baseline for next 4-12 weeks since macro-drivers move very slowly to capture short term trends. Predictive analysis and demand sensing to  refine short term forecasts-- plus scenario planning for best/worst case outcomes.
3. Market Intelligence overlay: Track competitor plant turnarounds/outages, new capacity start-up schedules etc.
4. S &OP - Cross functional process that reconciles the demand forecast against plant capacity, turnaround schedules and inventory targets.
5. Scenario planning/ margin modelling: rather than a single-point forecast -- because the actual business decision isn't just how much to make but "Is this margin environment good enough to even run this asset at all." So planners often model multiple feedstock-price and capacity scenarios rather than trusting one demand number.
